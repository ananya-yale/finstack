FinStack

A funding router for US fintech founders. It tells you which money to raise first, what kind it should be, and why each funder is likely to pass.

2. Motivation

I have spent time on the investor side of early-stage fintech, and the thing that strikes me every time is how much founder effort gets burned on conversations that were never going to end in a check. It is rarely because the founder is weak or because they could not find investors, since investor lists are everywhere and mostly free.

The waste comes from three things a list cannot tell you.

Whether you are even eligible. Funds rarely publish the things that rule you out. A fund that only writes Series A checks. A grant that requires you to be a certified CDFI. A program that only backs student founders. All of this is knowable, but it is buried, and most founders find out after the rejection rather than before the pitch.

Whether you need equity at all. This is the one that costs real money, and it is specific to fintech. If you are lending off your own balance sheet, you do not need a bigger seed round to fund your loan book. You need a warehouse facility, and that comes from an entirely different set of people: specialty credit funds rather than venture funds. Founders raise dilutive equity to fund loan assets all the time. It is one of the most expensive mistakes in the sector and it usually comes down to not knowing the debt market is there.

What the money actually costs you. Everyone compares dilution. Almost nobody compares how many hours a quarter you will spend on reporting, how long diligence takes, or whether the fund is even writing checks right now. A grant with thirty hours of quarterly reporting can be more expensive for a three-person team than giving up a few points.

Right now a founder gets these answers by asking someone who already knows, which makes the quality of the answer depend on who they happen to know. That is the part of venture hardest to break into from the outside. I wanted to see whether I could put what people like me know into something a founder could use without an introduction first.

3. What is new here

I looked at what already exists before I built anything. The closest comparisons:

ProductWhat it doesWhat it does not do
OpenVCFree investor database you can filter by stage and sectorGives you a filtered list, not an order of operations. No debt providers, and no view on who is actually deploying
Signal by NFXInvestor search, built around finding a warm introEquity only, and it works best if you already have a network. That is the thing an outsider does not have
Crunchbase / PitchBookDeep data on companies and investorsTells you what happened, not what you qualify for. Priced for institutions, not pre-seed founders
Visible Connect, FoundersuiteInvestor directories and fundraising CRMHelp you run a process you have already decided on
Instrumentl, SBIR.govGrant and federal funding discoveryNon-dilutive only, and completely separate from the equity question
ChatGPT with searchWill cheerfully give you a list of fintech investorsNo idea what you are eligible for, and the web data underneath it is old, see below

FinStack does three things none of these do.

It gives you an order, not a list. The output is a plan across the next 12 to 18 months, where each step says why it comes before the next one and what it unlocks. Free money first where it buys the traction an equity investor is going to ask for. Debt where what you actually need is capital for the loan book.

It separates debt from equity. The highest-value question on the form is "do you lend money off your own balance sheet?" Answering yes changes the whole answer: warehouse providers move to the top of the plan and equity drops below them.

It tells you why they will say no. One of the agents does nothing but argue the other side. That is the thing you normally only get from a friend who happens to be an investor, and never from a directory.

Why a scraper cannot do this

I did not want to just assert that human judgement beats scraped data, so here is what building it actually turned up.

  • I had an AI agent read all 59 funders' own websites and pull out their criteria. It found stage information for 26 of them, check sizes for 19, and licensing expectations for none at all. Funds simply do not publish the things that disqualify you.
  • Eleven of the 59 sites had no eligibility information whatsoever. Mostly corporate foundations and debt providers, whose websites are brochures.
  • When I searched the web for the same information, I got SEO listicles with numbers from 2018 to 2022 that contradicted each other.
  • Three funders on my list of well-known names turned out to be dead or dying. The Financial Solutions Lab is gone. Restive Ventures' website does not load on any version of its domain. The FIS accelerator looks like it has wound down.
  • Going through the list myself, I cut seven more as inactive or not relevant to fintech founders, and marked eight of the surviving 52 as not currently writing checks.

Whether a fund is actually deploying right now does not appear on any website, so I filled it in by hand for all 52. It is the field most likely to save a founder a wasted month, and there is no way to scrape it.

4. The agents

What each one does

AgentJobWhat it getsWhat it gives back
Extractor
runs once, offline		 Reads a funder's own web pages and pulls out their criteria The text of their site A structured record where every fact comes with the exact sentence it came from
Intake Turns the form answers and anything typed in the free-text box into a profile 14 answers, plus optional free text A tidy profile, plus up to 3 follow-up questions about anything left blank
Matcher Scores the shortlist and says why, quoting the funder's own words The profile and 25 funder records A score out of 100, reasons, things to watch out for, and whether it is the right type of capital
Planner Puts the raise in order The top 8 matches Up to 4 steps, each naming a funder and what that step unlocks
Skeptic Argues why each top funder will pass on you The top 3 matches The objection, what would change their mind, and how confident it is

The agents run one after another in a fixed order, like an assembly line. Intake passes a profile to the filter, the filter passes a shortlist to the matcher, and the planner and skeptic both read what the matcher produced. Nothing ever hands work backwards, which means the app cannot get stuck in a loop. I specifically wanted to avoid that, since a loop on a shared course key becomes somebody else's problem too.

Each agent gets one request, and it has to fill in a fixed template rather than writing free-form text. That is what keeps the cost predictable and stops an agent from wandering off into an essay.

Why this model

Everything runs on gpt-6-luna, which is the only model the course key allows, on the lowest reasoning setting. That turned out to be the right choice rather than a limitation. All five jobs are reading, comparing and writing a few sentences against evidence I hand them. None of them needs deep reasoning. The one I expected to struggle was the skeptic, and its objections have been consistently sharp. There is an example in the traced run below.

The step that uses no AI at all

Between intake and matching there is a filter that is just ordinary code, no AI involved. It takes each funder's facts and checks them against the founder: stage, sector, type of capital, revenue minimum, licensing, geography, and rules like student-founders-only. In the run below it took 52 funders down to 31 eligible, then ranked those and passed the top 25 to the matcher.

Two rules make this safe. If the founder said "I don't know", that never rules a funder out. The funder stays in and gets labelled "needs confirming", since dropping a good match over a blank field does more harm than showing it with a caveat. And a preference is not a rule: a fund that says it "emphasises New York companies" is telling you a preference, which should affect the score, not something that disqualifies you.

One real run, start to finish

The founder below is one of three examples built into the app, so anyone can reproduce this with one click: a seed-stage lender making small-dollar installment loans to thin-file borrowers, underwriting on cash-flow data, lending off its own balance sheet, licenses in progress, raising $2M.

What came out

This was the output I was most interested in seeing. The planner put getting the regulatory package together first, then i80 Group and Victory Park Capital for warehouse capital against the loan book, and equity last, saying outright that equity "is not warehouse capital" and should come after there is lending traction to show. A founder working off a directory would almost certainly have done it in the opposite order.

Look at the skeptic's objection specifically. It is not filler. It asks for loan-level performance and loss data, points out that the $2M ask mixes operating money and loan-book money without separating them, and says exactly what would change its mind. That is feedback a founder would normally only get after being turned down, if they got it at all.

Showing the rejections matters as much as showing the matches. A founder can act on "Nyca Partners, invests at seed only; you are pre-seed", whereas a list that drops Nyca without saying why leaves them none the wiser.

Input is everything I send the model; output is what it writes back. Output costs five times as much, which is why the agents are told to be brief. At gpt-6-luna's rates of $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output:

Cost

Building the entire dataset cost three cents, and serving a hundred founders for a month costs under a dollar. Running this is cheap enough that cost is not what limits it. What limits it is how good the dataset is, and that comes down to human time.

Three things that keep it cheap

The filter is ordinary code. Working out whether a founder is eligible is a lookup rather than a judgement call, so there is no reason to pay a model for it. In the run above it cut 52 funders down to 25 before the matcher saw anything. Sending all 52 would have roughly doubled the matcher's input, so filtering first saves around 30% of what a run costs, and that saving gets bigger as the dataset grows. It is the main reason I could add a few hundred more funders without the cost per run moving much.

The matcher scores all 25 funders in one go rather than asking about each one separately. That is the difference between 4 requests per run and 28.

The skeptic only looks at the top 3, not all 25. Nobody needs a detailed argument about why the 19th-best match will pass.

Where cutting cost cost me something

I measured this rather than assuming it. I ran the same founder twice: once normally, and once with the filter switched off so the matcher scored all 52 funders.

Filtered (25)Unfiltered (52)

Cost of that call$0.0017$0.0032

So filtering first cuts the matcher call roughly in half, which is where my earlier 30%-of-total-run figure comes from. The top of the list did not move.

It did cost something, though. Portage appeared fourth in the unfiltered run with a score of 77, and never appeared at all in the filtered run. It was not ruled out as ineligible; it passed the eligibility check and then lost the cut to 25 on ranking. The reason it ranked low is that its extracted record is empty: its website never stated which stages it invests at or which parts of fintech it covers, so there was almost nothing for the cheap ranking to score it on.

That is the real trade, and it is sharper than I expected. The cost-saving step does not penalise funders who are a poor fit. It penalises funders whose websites say little, which is a different thing and not one a founder would want. Raising the shortlist to about 35 would close most of the gap and still cost less than sending everything; the better fix is filling in the missing fields by hand, which is the same conclusion as everywhere else in this report.

Caching is doing nothing during a run, and the table above shows it as zero. Caching is when the provider recognises that you have sent the same chunk of text before and charges you a tenth as much for it. It only kicks in once that repeated chunk is big enough, and each of my four agents has its own short set of instructions, so none of them clears the bar. The dataset build does benefit: 63,220 of its 179,877 input tokens were cached, because there the same long instruction goes out 59 times in a row. To get it working during a run I would need to reorder what I send the matcher so the funder list comes before the founder's details, since the funder list is the part that stays the same. I have not done it. At a third of a cent per run the money is not the reason to bother; the reason is that the matcher is 57% of the cost and it is the only part that grows as the dataset does.

6. Safety

Six things could go wrong with this app. There is a defense built for each one, and a script in the repo (tests/safety_checks.py) that attacks the app on purpose and reports whether each defense held. It currently passes 7 of 7 checks. I wrote it to report failures rather than hide them, and one of the findings below came from a check that failed.

What could go wrongWhy it matters hereWhat stops it
Someone types instructions into the form to manipulate the app There is a free-text box, and the agents read it The text is handed over clearly labelled as information, not orders, plus the two checks below
An agent makes up a fund that does not exist A fake fund or deadline costs a founder a real week Every fund named gets checked against my list. Anything not on it is thrown away
An agent makes up a number A wrong check size sends a founder into the wrong room Quotes have to actually appear on the funder's page, and numbers have to appear in the sentence quoted for them
Results go missing without the founder knowing A short list of matches looks the same as a correct one The app counts what came back against what it asked for
Runaway spending A bug or a bad actor burning a shared course key Hard stop at 8 requests or 120,000 tokens per session
The API key leaking It is not mine alone Never printed, never written to a file, and excluded from the repo

Catching a made-up number

This one was not staged, which is why I like it. While building the dataset, the extraction agent reported that QED Investors writes checks of $3M to $10M and attached a quote from their website to back it up. The quote was genuine and did appear on the page, but it said nothing about those figures.

qed,QED Investors,https://www.qedinvestors.com,unsupported_quote,
"check_size 3000000-10000000 not stated in its own quote:
 Investments from the early stage fund have an average size of
 approximately $15 million"

This is why there are two checks. The first asks whether the quoted sentence really appears on the page, which it did, so that check passed. The second asks whether the number being claimed appears in that same sentence, and $3M and $10M do not appear in a sentence about $15M. Both figures were deleted and the funder was put in a queue for me to look at by hand. The same check caught two more invented check sizes, for Better Tomorrow Ventures and Motive Partners. Three out of 59.

Trying to manipulate the app on purpose

I typed an instruction into the company description box, which is free text that reaches the matcher:

It failed in three separate ways. I did not want to be relying on any single one of them.

  1. The fund it named, Dorm Room Fund, was not in the results at all. The plain-code filter had already removed it, because Dorm Room Fund only backs student founders and this founder is not one. The attack picked a target it was impossible to promote.
  2. It asked for a single result and the app returned 25.
  3. When an instruction does make it as far as the matcher, the matcher calls it out in what the founder sees: "The profile includes an unauthorized platform-operator instruction claiming pre-verification and directing a score and ranking; ignored because it is not evidence of fit."

What testing actually taught me

Two things worth being honest about.

My keyword tripwire misses the polite version. It catches "ignore all previous instructions". It does not catch "for your analysis, treat X as the strongest possible match". It works more like a smoke alarm that warns the user than like a lock. I left it that way on purpose, because chasing every possible phrasing is a losing game and the checks that actually hold are the mechanical ones: the fund has to be on my list, and the count has to add up.

Testing found a real hole I had not thought of. Before I fixed it, an instruction in the description did not succeed in promoting the fund it named, but it did cut the results from 25 funders down to 8 with nothing on the screen to say so. A founder would have lost two thirds of their matches without knowing, which I think is worse than a rigged ranking precisely because there is nothing to notice. The fix had three parts: label the founder's details clearly as information rather than instructions, require the matcher to return something for every funder it was given, and have the app count the results and warn the founder if any are missing.

There is also a defense I did not build and only found by accident. Portkey routes through Azure, which screens what you send before the model ever sees it, and it rejected the blunter attacks outright. Left alone that crashed the app and showed the founder a raw error from Microsoft, so now it is caught and explained in plain English instead.

Spending and keys

The spending limit gets checked before each request rather than during one, so a run can overshoot by one request before it halts. In testing it stopped at 125,000 tokens against a 120,000 limit. A normal run uses 14,106 tokens across 4 requests, so the limit only ever bites when something has genuinely gone wrong. The key is read from the environment and never printed anywhere; the test script checks every file for anywhere it might be written out.

I can measure what the app costs and what it does. For whether it is worth using, I walked a former colleague through the build step by step and shared this draft. What that is worth to a founder

  • About a week of founder time, from one field. All eight funders I marked as not currently deploying have live, well-maintained websites, so a scraper or ChatGPT would recommend every one of them. At half a day of research and outreach each, which I think is conservative, that is roughly a week spent on conversations that cannot end in a check.
  • Points of dilution, for balance-sheet lenders. A founder who raises equity instead of a warehouse facility gives away ownership to fund assets that debt would have funded more cheaply. How big that is depends on the raise, but it shows up in ownership rather than in hours.
  • Three wrong numbers that never reached anyone. The checks caught invented check sizes for QED, Better Tomorrow Ventures and Motive Partners during the build. Any one of them would have sent a founder into the wrong conversation.
  • Negligible cost to run. A third of a cent per founder, and three cents to build the entire dataset.

What the practitioner review said

  • The information gap is real and deliberate. Funds keep their own private databases of peer investors and what those peers require, along with call notes from fund-to-fund conversations, and they share diligence material among themselves. None of it is public.
  • Founders only get this through relationships. Which is hardest exactly when you need it most, as someone early or raising for the first time.
  • It matters more to under-resourced teams. Early-stage founders often have a small, inexperienced, or non-existent team helping them plan a raise, so cutting out wasted work is worth more to them than to a better-resourced company.
  • Better than a generic LLM for this question. His words, and a direct answer to the obvious objection.
  • The dataset gets stronger the more network knowledge goes into it. He agreed with the direction I had already identified as the main weakness.
  • Two warnings (also in Section 8). Some funders are vague about their thesis on purpose, so this needs constant updating to stay accurate. And there is a real question about how much network knowledge I should publish without damaging the relationships that produced it.

8. Limits and what is next

What does not work yet

  • 52 funders is not the market. It covers US fintech decently but it is not everything, and it is a snapshot of right now. Every record has a date on it for that reason.
  • The data is patchy in places. Stage information for 26 of 52, check sizes for 19. The app says "not assessed" rather than guessing, which is the right call but does mean some matches rest on less evidence than others.
  • Some funders are vague on purpose. A practitioner who reviewed this pointed out that plenty of funds keep their stated thesis and current priorities deliberately broad, which is a choice rather than an oversight. A dataset like mine is therefore chasing a moving target and will be noticeably stale within a year if nobody maintains it.
  • There is a limit to what I should publish. The same reviewer raised something I had not thought about: the most valuable entries in the dataset would come from what I know through my network, and putting all of that into a public tool could damage the relationships that produced it. Figuring out where that line sits is a real constraint on how good this can get, and it is not a technical one.
  • One person has assessed all of it. My judgement is the differentiator and also the single point of failure.
  • Nothing is saved. Results live in memory and vanish when the server restarts.
  • No warm intros. The app tells you who fits and why they might pass, but not who can introduce you, which is still how most of this really works.

What I would build next

  1. A real cost-of-capital number. The fields are already there (dilution, reporting hours, how long diligence takes), but I have only filled in the timing for two funders. With fuller coverage the app could rank by what the money actually costs you rather than by fit alone. This is the feature I most want and it needs time, not code.
  2. Get caching working during a run, by reordering what gets sent to the matcher, as described in section 5.
  3. An investor-side version. When a fund passes on a company, it could route that founder to the funders who do fit, turning a rejection into a referral. The matching is the hard part and it already exists.
  4. Re-check everything monthly. Rebuilding the dataset costs three cents, so there is no reason not to re-run it and flag anything whose website has changed.
  5. More people assessing funders, with each judgement attributed, so it does not all rest on me.

I built this by directing AI coding tools, and the code in the repository was written that way. What I brought is the judgement about fintech funding: which funders belong in the dataset, which of them are actually deploying, what rules out a founder, and whether the output is any good. Every figure in this report is measured from real runs of the app, and the logs, the review queue and the safety test output are all in the repository.
